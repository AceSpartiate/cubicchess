The board stays orbitable while the computer thinks. Drag to orbit · scroll to zoom. Click a piece, click a destination to see its new range, then Confirm (Enter) or Cancel (Esc). Files a–h, ranks 1–8, levels L1 (floor) to L8.
Movement in three axes

Pawn — one step forward, or one step straight up; never down. First move only: two forward or two up. Captures on any forward diagonal, including forward+up and forward+down.

Promotion — a pawn promotes when it reaches the far rank, on any level, and you choose freely between queen, rook, bishop and knight as in ordinary chess. Climbing to the top level is just a move; it no longer promotes.

Knight — the 2+1 leap on any pair of axes: 24 destinations, jumps over everything.

Bishop — 20 rays. Twelve are the 45° face diagonals, where two axes change at once; the other eight are true triagonals, where all three change together and it moves corner to corner through the cube. That second family is what makes it dangerous: a two-axis diagonal preserves the parity of x+y+z, so a bishop without triagonals is locked to half the cube forever, exactly like a light-squared bishop in flat chess. The triagonal flips that parity, so this bishop reaches all 512 cells and averages 38.5 squares of range against a rook's 21.

Rook — slides along one axis: forward, back, side to side, up, down.

Queen — every ray a king could start: 6 straight, 12 diagonal, 8 triagonal. She owns the cube.

King — one step to any of 26 neighbours. Check, checkmate and stalemate all apply.

The computer — levels 1–2 look one or two plies ahead and pick loosely, so they blunder. Levels 3–6 run alpha-beta with quiescence, null-move pruning, late-move reductions and a transposition table, reaching depth 3 to about 7. Its evaluation is built from this cube's own geometry rather than from flat chess: a rook reaches exactly 21 squares from anywhere here, so it gets no bonus for centralising, while a knight swings between 6 and 24 and is scored steeply for it.

Piece values — measured from mobility in the empty cube, with the pawn as the unit: pawn 1, rook 6, knight 6, bishop 11, queen 18. Knight and rook really do land in the same place here; the unrounded figures are 6.2 and 5.8. The triagonal roughly doubles the bishop, putting it second only to the queen — losing one is closer to losing two rooks than to a knight trade. The running total sits beside the captured pieces.

Castling — along the floor rank as in standard chess: king and rook both unmoved, the squares between them empty, and the king neither starting in check nor crossing an attacked square. Any vertical move by either piece forfeits the right. No en passant in this variant.
